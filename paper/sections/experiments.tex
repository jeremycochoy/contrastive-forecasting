\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

On GIFT-Eval, we compare our model with value-space models trained
with the rest of our recipe.\footnote{Code, training scripts
and experiment notebooks are available at
\url{https://github.com/jeremycochoy/contrastive-forecasting}.}

\paragraph{Our models.}
Our models are the model of Section~\ref{sec:method}, trained with the
loss of Equation~\eqref{eq:loss-total} on the data of
Section~\ref{sec:data}, at two sizes: $\dmodel = 64$ with $1$ encoder
layer and $3$ forecaster layers, and $\dmodel = 384$ with $1$ encoder
layer and $5$ forecaster layers. The muP base stays at width $64$, so
the learning rate transfers. They train with AdamW ($\beta_1 = 0.9$,
$\beta_2 = 0.98$) and a weight decay of $0.1$, for the budget below.
The learning rate is $10^{-3}$, then an exponential cooldown halves it
every $5{,}000$ steps, from step $135{,}000$ at width $64$ and from
step $125{,}000$ at width $384$. The forecast head of Section~\ref{sec:forecast-head} then trains
on the frozen backbone for $30{,}000$ steps at batch size $256$, with
AdamW ($\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.999$), a constant learning rate of
$10^{-3}$, a weight decay of $0.01$ and a gradient norm clipped at $1$.

\paragraph{Moirai-style models.}
To measure the effect of the latent-space objective, we train
value-space models with the rest of our recipe: the same data,
normalisation, time-scale embedding, GRU patch embedding and
transformer blocks. Their patches come from
Moirai~\citep{woo2024moirai}: they have one patch embedding and one
output head for each of the patch sizes $8$, $16$, $32$, $64$ and
$128$, and each series draws its patch size from the range of its
frequency.
Each output head predicts the $9$ quantiles of the next patch, and the
loss is the pinball loss on the normalised values. Like our model,
they also run on their own forecasts: the model reads its median
forecast up to $3$ times, and the loss adds the pinball loss of each
depth. The learning rate rises to $10^{-3}$ over $10{,}000$ steps,
then a cosine takes it to $0$ at step $166{,}000$. We train one model
of the size of ours, at width $64$ with $1$ encoder layer and $3$
forecaster layers. We train a bigger one at width $384$ with $3$
encoder layers and $3$ forecaster layers. It has $14.4$ million
parameters, the size of Moirai small. The baselines differ from our
run in a few more ways: they clip the gradient norm at $1$, they skip
the windows with NaN values or extreme spikes, and they run the
attention, the feed-forward network and the convolution in fp32,
where our models run them in fp16 at width $64$ and in bf16 at width
$384$. The width-64 baseline also keeps
the early targets that our losses skip
(Section~\ref{sec:normalisation}).

\paragraph{Training budget.}
All four models train for $166{,}000$ steps at batch size $256$:
$251$ real windows, the forked pair and the crossfade triplet
(Section~\ref{sec:data}). The models read $42$ million windows of the
pretraining corpus, or $18.5\%$ of its values.

\paragraph{Metric.}
For each of the $97$ configurations, we compute the MASE of the median
forecast, with the seasonal period of the frequency, and we divide it
by the MASE of the seasonal naive forecast. The GM-MASE is the
geometric mean of this ratio over the $97$ configurations. Lower is
better, and the seasonal naive forecast scores $1$. Each score comes
from one training run.
